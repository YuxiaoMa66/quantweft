## 1. Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

1. **Calculate descriptive statistics and distinguish their conventions.** Keep simple returns separate from log returns, and arithmetic averages separate from the compound growth rate (CAGR), a geometric average. Calculate annualized volatility, per-period and annualized Sharpe ratios, the Sortino ratio, maximum drawdown (MaxDD), the Calmar ratio, hit rate, Profit Factor, tracking error, and information ratio; connect these measures to the turnover recorded in the actual ledger in Chapter 29.
2. **Make statistical inferences under non-normality (PSR).** Understand how higher moments—skewness $\gamma_3$ and kurtosis $\gamma_4$—inflate the estimated variance of the Sharpe ratio; use the Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio (PSR) formula and its one-sided hypothesis test; and distinguish the probability of skill from the probability of making money.
3. **Account for selection bias and multiple testing (DSR).** Understand how the maximum Sharpe ratio is inflated when the best result is selected from $N$ independent trials; derive the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) and its threshold $\mathrm{SR}_0$; and understand the heuristic estimate of the effective number of independent trials when trials are correlated.
4. **Use Combinatorially Symmetric Cross-Validation and the Probability of Backtest Overfitting (CSCV/PBO).** Understand the matrix-partition algorithm, relative rank $\bar{\omega}$, and the Logit transform $\lambda$; distinguish the diagnostic role of CSCV from that of a time-ordered deployment backtest (Walk-Forward).
5. **Recognize inference failure conditions and empirical checks.** Understand variance inflation due to serial dependence, heavy tails, and extreme regime changes, and apply the full inference workflow to the Kenneth French market data.

## 2. Why Quant Needs This

One of the most common illusions in a quantitative research lifecycle is the “strategy champion trap.” A researcher tries 200 combinations of moving-average periods, exit thresholds, or cross-sectional factor weights, then selects a “champion strategy” with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 2.5. The researcher reports to the investment committee with great excitement, claiming overwhelming statistical significance.

Yet six months after deployment, the strategy suffers a severe drawdown. Why can a perfect historical backtest fail to reproduce its performance in live trading?

Classical financial statistics textbooks often assume that asset returns are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) normal observations and test one prespecified strategy once. Modern quantitative research faces two unavoidable violations:

1. **Hidden risk from non-normal returns.** Hedge funds and quantitative strategies—especially mean-reversion, leveraged arbitrage, and short-volatility strategies—often exhibit negative skew and high excess kurtosis (heavy tails). Ignoring negative skew and heavy tails can substantially understate the estimation variance of the Sharpe ratio and greatly overstate statistical significance.
2. **Selection bias from multiple hypothesis tests.** Even if all 200 candidate models are generated by pure Gaussian white noise with no predictive power, the expected maximum sample Sharpe ratio is naturally greater than zero. Reporting only the winning point estimate without disclosing the trial search ledger is a classic form of data snooping.

A quantitative researcher therefore needs inferential statistics as well as descriptive return measures: use PSR to measure estimation uncertainty under stated moment conditions, DSR to raise the comparison threshold after a search, and CSCV to examine the relative out-of-sample ranks of candidates selected in sample.

## 3. Intuition

### 3.1 A Coin-Toss Stock-Picking Contest

Imagine a contest with 1,000 participants, each of whom tosses a coin 10 times. A head counts as a win and a tail as a loss.

For one fair-coin participant, the probability of getting 10 heads in a row is $p=2^{-10}$. The expected number of perfect records among 1,000 participants is $1000p=0.9765625$. This does not mean that exactly one participant will win every contest. If participants are also independent, the probability that at least one gets 10 heads is $1-(1-2^{-10})^{1000}\approx0.623576$. If that “champion” claims to have a special ability to predict the future, an investor who sees only the champion’s 10-for-10 record (the point estimate) may be impressed. An investor who sees the full list of 1,000 participants (the trial ledger) will recognize that the perfect record can arise as an extreme value from random sampling.

Searching for quantitative strategies works the same way:

- **PSR intuition:** If a participant earns very little on heads but loses heavily on tails (negative skew and heavy tails), the confidence band around the long-run Sharpe ratio is wide even if the win rate is respectable.
- **DSR intuition:** Given $N$ entrants, extreme-value theory raises the comparison threshold $\mathrm{SR}_0$ under the zero-skill hypothesis. Exceeding the threshold is statistical evidence against that hypothesis, but does not prove repeatable true alpha.
- **CSCV intuition:** Divide history into blocks. If the champion selected on some blocks tends to rank poorly relative to other candidates on the remaining blocks, the “optimization” may have selected a candidate that performs worse out of sample. The strict definition uses $\bar r<N/2$; CSCV also uses $\bar r\le(N+1)/2$.

## 4. Mathematical Setup

### 4.1 Notation and Assumptions

- **Return intervals:** Equal-length discrete periods $t=1,2,\dots,T$, with per-period simple returns $r_t\in(-1,+\infty)$ while the account is solvent. Wealth calculations also allow the boundary $r_t=-1$: without external capital, account wealth is then absorbed at zero and later multiplicative returns cannot revive it. A simple return below −100% requires a different negative-wealth model and is rejected by the current interface.
- **Annualization factor:** Let $A$ be the number of equally spaced periods in a year (daily $A=250$, monthly $A=12$).
- **Benchmark and risk-free series:** A risk-free return vector $r_{f,t}$ and benchmark portfolio return vector $r_{B,t}$, both at the same frequency and of equal length.
- **Excess returns:** $r_{e,t}=r_t-r_{f,t}$. Their sample mean and standard deviation at the same frequency are
  \[
  \hat{\mu}_e=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T r_{e,t},\qquad
  \hat{\sigma}_e=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^T(r_{e,t}-\hat{\mu}_e)^2}.
  \]
- **PSR moments:** The PSR implementation estimates skewness and Pearson’s raw kurtosis from excess returns. `psr_from_returns` uses bias-corrected skewness and `scipy.stats.kurtosis` with `fisher=False` and `bias=False`. The normal benchmark has raw kurtosis $\gamma_4=3$, not excess kurtosis.

---

### 4.2 Inputs, Assumptions, and Interpretation

For a high-Sharpe curve, first ask what happened, then ask how much evidence it provides relative to a threshold. Convert frequencies before substituting values; do not add an annualized label after the calculation.

| Input or output | Frequency and units | Conditions and interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $r_t,r_{f,t},r_{e,t}$ | Same observation period; dimensionless simple returns | Subtract contemporaneous observations; do not subtract an annual risk-free rate directly from monthly returns |
| $\widehat{SR},SR^*,SR_0$ | Per-period, dimensionless ratios at the same frequency | The observed value and comparison threshold use the same scale |
| $n,\gamma_3,\gamma_4$ | Number of observations at the same frequency; dimensionless skewness and Pearson raw kurtosis | Normal raw kurtosis is 3; matching two moments does not prove normality |
| PSR standard error | Standard error of the per-period Sharpe ratio | Assumes i.i.d. observations, finite fourth moment, and an asymptotic approximation; serial dependence cannot be fixed by merely increasing $n$ |
| $V[\widehat{SR}],N$ | Sharpe variance at the same frequency; number of trials | Variance scales with the square of the Sharpe frequency conversion; $N$ depends on an independent-search model and is not automatically the number of candidate columns |
| PSR, DSR, PBO | Dimensionless tail area or fraction of partitions | PSR is threshold inference, DSR is conditional calibration, and PBO is a candidate-rank diagnostic; they answer different questions |

If $SR_{ann}=\sqrt A\,SR$, then $V[SR_{ann}]=A\,V[SR]$. For example, with an annualized Sharpe of 2.5 and 250 trading days per year, first compute the daily Sharpe $2.5/\sqrt{250}=0.158114$, then compare it with the daily threshold 0.113172; the difference at the same frequency is 0.044942. Compute the standard error from the native daily returns first. If these already computed quantities are then displayed consistently in annualized units, multiply the observed value, threshold, and standard error by $\sqrt{250}$; $z$ is unchanged. Do not leave daily $n$ and moments unchanged while substituting an annualized Sharpe into the per-period $D$ formula. Changing only the observed Sharpe creates spurious significance (32-L02).

The annualization convention follows from sums of returns: under independence, identical distribution, and finite variance, the mean of an $A$-period sum is $A\mu$, its standard deviation is $\sqrt A\,\sigma$, and their ratio is $\sqrt A\,\mu/\sigma$. Normality is not required for this scaling identity. Actual annual wealth from simple returns is $\prod_t(1+r_t)$, not $1+\sum_t r_t$, so this Sharpe annualization does not replace CAGR. Serial dependence requires adding the covariances across lags (32-L04).

## 5. Formal Definitions

### 5.1 Descriptive Measures

1. **Wealth index and CAGR.** Starting from initial capital $W_0>0$, the compounded wealth path is
   \[
   W_t=W_0\prod_{i=1}^t(1+r_i).
   \]
   Over a span of $Y$ years, the compound annual growth rate is
   $$\mathrm{CAGR}=\left(\frac{W_T}{W_0}\right)^{1/Y}-1.$$
   CAGR equals −100% if and only if bankruptcy occurs ($W_T=0$).

2. **Maximum drawdown (MaxDD) and Calmar ratio.** The historical wealth peak at time $t$ is $M_t=\max_{0\le s\le t}W_s$. The drawdown series is $D_t=(W_t-M_t)/M_t\in[-1,0]$. Maximum drawdown is its nonnegative absolute value, $\mathrm{MaxDD}=\max_t|D_t|$. The Calmar ratio is
   $$\mathrm{Calmar}=\frac{\mathrm{CAGR}}{\mathrm{MaxDD}}.$$
   If $\mathrm{MaxDD}=0$, Calmar is undefined and an error is raised.

3. **Per-period and annualized Sharpe ratios.** The per-period Sharpe ratio is defined at the native frequency of the return series and has no annualization factor:
   $$\hat{\mathrm{SR}}=\frac{\hat{\mu}_e}{\hat{\sigma}_e}.$$
   The conventional annualized Sharpe ratio is $\hat{\mathrm{SR}}_{\mathrm{ann}}=\sqrt A\,\hat{\mathrm{SR}}$.

4. **Sortino ratio and downside deviation.** Let the target return threshold be $\tau$ at the same frequency. The full-sample downside deviation includes all $n$ observations:
   $$\mathrm{DD}_\tau=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^n\min(r_t-\tau,0)^2}.$$
   The per-period Sortino ratio is $(\bar r-\tau)/\mathrm{DD}_\tau$. Its annualized form conventionally multiplies by $\sqrt A$; downside deviation and the target do not automatically scale according to the independent-sum standard-deviation theorem. If downside deviation is zero, the ratio is undefined. The target must be finite.

5. **Hit rate and Profit Factor.**
   - **Strict hit rate, excluding flat periods:**
     $$\mathrm{HitRate}=\frac{\sum\mathbf{1}_{\{r_t>0\}}}{\sum\mathbf{1}_{\{r_t\ne0\}}}.$$
   - **Profit Factor, the ratio of total gains to the absolute value of total losses:**
     $$\mathrm{ProfitFactor}=\frac{\sum_{r_t>0}r_t}{|\sum_{r_t<0}r_t|}.$$
     This chapter gives each period equal weight. This is neither average winning return divided by average losing return nor a transaction Profit Factor calculated from actual monetary trade P&L. If there are no losses, Profit Factor is undefined. The hit-rate denominator excludes zero-return periods; it is also undefined when every period is flat.

6. **Tracking error and information ratio.** Active returns are $r_{a,t}=r_t-r_{B,t}$. Annualized tracking error is $\mathrm{TE}=\hat{\sigma}(r_a)\sqrt A$. The annualized information ratio is $\mathrm{IR}=(\bar r_a/\hat{\sigma}(r_a))\sqrt A$.

7. **Ledger turnover.** Connect this measure to the actual backtest ledger in Chapter 29. Turnover for one rebalance is total traded notional divided by pre-trade NAV:
   $$\mathrm{Turnover}_t=\frac{\mathrm{traded\_notional}_t}{\mathrm{nav\_pre\_trade}_t}.$$

### 5.2 Inference Measures

1. **Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio (PSR).** Under i.i.d. returns with a finite fourth moment, skewness and heavy tails adjust the estimated variance of the sample per-period Sharpe ratio. For a same-frequency benchmark $\mathrm{SR}^*$,
   $$\mathrm{PSR}(\mathrm{SR}^*)=
   \Phi\left(
   \frac{(\hat{\mathrm{SR}}-\mathrm{SR}^*)\sqrt{n-1}}
   {\sqrt{1-\hat{\gamma}_3\hat{\mathrm{SR}}+
   \frac{\hat{\gamma}_4-1}{4}\hat{\mathrm{SR}}^2}}
   \right).$$

2. **Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR).** When the best Sharpe ratio is selected from $N$ independent trials, the expected maximum random Sharpe threshold under the zero-skill hypothesis is
   $$\mathrm{SR}_0=\sqrt{V[\{\hat{\mathrm{SR}}_n\}]}
   \left((1-\gamma)\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)
   +\gamma\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{Ne}\right)\right),$$
   where $\gamma\approx0.5772156649$ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. This uses an independent Gaussian Sharpe approximation and applies to integer $N\ge2$. Even at $N=2$ it is an approximation: the exact expected maximum of two standard normal variables is $1/\sqrt{\pi}\approx0.564190$, while the approximation gives 0.519755. With $N=1$ there is no search premium; the zero-mean noise threshold is 0, and one can use a single-test PSR. The current DSR API explicitly requires $N\ge2$ and does not substitute 1 into the inverse CDF. The expected maximum is a comparison threshold, not a critical value controlling the family-wise error rate of the whole search. DSR is not automatically an exact post-selection p-value. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio is
   $$\mathrm{DSR}=\mathrm{PSR}(\mathrm{SR}_0).$$

3. **Combinatorially Symmetric Cross-Validation (CSCV) and PBO.** Divide the synchronized $T\times N$ candidate return matrix by rows into an even number $S$ of equal-sized blocks. Choose any $S/2$ blocks for the in-sample set $J$ and use the complementary blocks as the out-of-sample set $\bar J$. There are $C(S,S/2)$ combinations.
   
   Select the best strategy in sample:
   \[
   n^*=\arg\max_n\hat{\mathrm{SR}}_{n,\mathrm{IS}}.
   \]
   Rank all $N$ strategies in the test set, with the best ranked $N$. The test-set relative rank and Logit of the strategy selected in sample are
   \[
   \bar{\omega}_c=\frac{\bar r_{n^*}}{N+1},\qquad
   \lambda_c=\ln\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_c}{1-\bar{\omega}_c}\right).
   \]
   The current CSCV convention counts partitions whose test-set relative rank is at or below the midpoint: $\lambda\le0\iff\bar r\le(N+1)/2$. The local PBO Definition 2.2 instead uses $\bar r<N/2$. These are different thresholds and may produce different counts for even $N$ as well; the difference is not merely an equality convention at one shared threshold.
   $$\mathrm{PBO}_{\mathrm{CSCV}}=
   \frac{1}{C(S,S/2)}\sum_c\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda_c\le0\}}.$$

## 6. Derivations

### 6.1 Algebra for the PSR Denominator Variance Term

Following Mertens (2002) and Bailey & López de Prado (2012a, Equation 8), the asymptotic variance expansion of the sample Sharpe ratio for non-normal i.i.d. returns is
$$\sigma^2_{\hat{\mathrm{SR}}}
=1+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{SR}^2-\gamma_3\mathrm{SR}
+\frac{\gamma_4-3}{4}\mathrm{SR}^2.$$
Consider the two polynomial terms involving $\mathrm{SR}^2$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{SR}^2+\frac{\gamma_4-3}{4}\mathrm{SR}^2
&=\frac{2}{4}\mathrm{SR}^2+\frac{\gamma_4-3}{4}\mathrm{SR}^2\\
&=\frac{\gamma_4-3+2}{4}\mathrm{SR}^2\\
&=\frac{\gamma_4-1}{4}\mathrm{SR}^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Substituting this identity into the asymptotic variance expression gives
$$\sigma^2_{\hat{\mathrm{SR}}}
=1-\gamma_3\mathrm{SR}+\frac{\gamma_4-1}{4}\mathrm{SR}^2.$$
The expression above is the asymptotic variance coefficient before scaling by sample size; it is not the finite-sample variance itself. Equation (11) uses an $n-1$ denominator and substitutes sample moments to obtain the following standard-error estimate. This approximation does not become an exact finite-sample result merely because of the Bessel correction:
$$\hat{\sigma}_{\hat{\mathrm{SR}}}
=\sqrt{\frac{1-\hat{\gamma}_3\hat{\mathrm{SR}}
+\frac{\hat{\gamma}_4-1}{4}\hat{\mathrm{SR}}^2}{n-1}}.$$
Under normality, $\gamma_3=0$ and $\gamma_4=3$, so the denominator term reduces to the classical Lo (2002) expression $\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2}\hat{\mathrm{SR}}^2}$. Matching these two moments does not establish that returns are normally distributed. When $\hat{\mathrm{SR}}>0$, more negative skewness or larger raw kurtosis increases the variance term; the direction of the PSR change also depends on which side of the threshold the observed Sharpe lies, as discussed below.

The financial interpretation is to estimate the error in “mean divided by volatility” before comparing it with a threshold. Let
$$D=1-\gamma_3\mathrm{SR}+\frac{(\gamma_4-1)\mathrm{SR}^2}{4}.$$
Then the estimator’s standard error is approximately $\sqrt{D/(n-1)}$; subtracting the threshold from the observed value and dividing by this standard error gives the PSR $z$-statistic. Increasing $n$ only reduces sampling error within a model whose conditions hold. It cannot make a nonexistent population fourth moment finite or make dependent observations independent.

The direction relative to the threshold also matters. For a fixed positive Sharpe, a moment change that increases $D$ moves PSR toward 0.5: if the observed Sharpe is above the threshold, $z$ falls and PSR moves down toward 0.5; if it is below, the negative $z$ rises toward zero and PSR moves up toward 0.5; if it equals the threshold, PSR remains 0.5. Thus, “heavy tails always lower PSR” is missing the condition that the observed value is above the threshold (32-E02).

### 6.2 Extreme-Value Approximation for the Maximum of Independent Standard Normals

Let $Z_1,Z_2,\dots,Z_N\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, and let $M_N=\max(Z_1,\dots,Z_N)$. Its cumulative distribution function is $F_{M_N}(x)=[\Phi(x)]^N$ and its density is $f_{M_N}(x)=N\phi(x)[\Phi(x)]^{N-1}$.

The exact expected value can be calculated by numerical integration:
$$E[M_N]=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}x\,N\phi(x)[\Phi(x)]^{N-1}\,dx.$$
By extreme-value theory (the Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem), standardized normal maxima converge weakly to a Gumbel distribution. Bailey & López de Prado (2014) give the following large-sample analytic approximation using the Euler–Mascheroni constant $\gamma\approx0.5772$:
$$E[M_N]\approx(1-\gamma)\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)
+\gamma\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{Ne}\right).$$
The CLI output `expected_max_standard_normal_comparison` reports:

- $N=10$: exact value $1.538753$, BLdP approximation $1.574598$, difference $+0.035846$;
- $N=50$: exact value $2.249074$, BLdP approximation $2.276303$, difference $+0.027229$;
- $N=100$: exact value $2.507594$, BLdP approximation $2.530603$, difference $+0.023009$.

The absolute error in these three examples decreases as $N$ grows. The acceptable error depends on the required precision; the approximation must not be described as an exact expectation.

## 7. Financial Interpretation

### 7.1 Why PSR Is Not the “Probability of Making Money”

Beginners sometimes misread $\mathrm{PSR}(0)=0.95$ as “there is a 95% probability that this strategy will make money in the future.” That is a serious statistical error.

Under i.i.d. returns with a finite fourth moment and an asymptotic normal approximation for the Sharpe estimator, $\mathrm{PSR}(\mathrm{SR}^*)$ is the normal tail area for a threshold comparison. For the one-sided null $H_0:\mathrm{SR}\le\mathrm{SR}^*$, at the boundary $\mathrm{SR}=\mathrm{SR}^*$, the corresponding approximate $p$-value is $1-\mathrm{PSR}$. Thus PSR can be expressed as a one-sided frequentist confidence level at a prespecified significance level. It is not a posterior probability about a fixed unknown parameter.

PSR measures statistical evidence for the estimated Sharpe relative to a specified threshold; it does not give the probability of a future profit-and-loss path. A strategy with 99% PSR and a small positive expected return can still have a per-period probability of loss close to 50%.

### 7.2 The Trial Ledger and “Alpha from Nothing”

In the chapter’s zero-skill simulation, $M=200$ independent noise strategies each have $T=1250$ daily return observations. For seed `20261032`, the champion’s annualized Sharpe is 1.2318 and its naive one-sided $p$-value is 0.002941. Yet the annualized DSR threshold is 1.3257 (daily 0.083843) and DSR is 0.417160. These are results for that simulation and the DSR assumptions; they neither prove nor disprove alpha for any real strategy.

### 8.2 Arithmetic and Geometric Averages at Opposite Extremes (Volatility Drag)

Consider two period returns: +50% in the first period and −50% in the second (initial capital 1.0):

- Ending capital: $W_2=1.0\times1.5\times0.5=0.75$ (a 25% loss);
- Arithmetic average: $(0.50+(-0.50))/2=0.00\%$ (`hand_descriptive_metrics.arithmetic_vs_geometric.arithmetic_mean`);
- Geometric average: $\sqrt{1.5\times0.5}-1=\sqrt{0.75}-1=-13.3975\%$ (`hand_descriptive_metrics.arithmetic_vs_geometric.geometric_mean`).

**Financial takeaway:** A zero arithmetic average does not mean the account broke even. High volatility can impose a severe compounding drag (volatility drag), so asset allocation should be evaluated using compound geometric growth.

## 12. Common Mistakes

### 12.1 Error 1: Annualizing Volatility with $\sqrt A$ for Serially Correlated Returns

- **Example of the error and why it occurs:** When the full weighted autocorrelation sum of asset returns is positive (as can occur with illiquid assets or smoothed private-equity valuations), one-period variance does not accumulate as if periods were independent. Blindly multiplying by $\sqrt A$ can materially understate true annualized portfolio volatility and inflate the annualized Sharpe ratio.
- **Executable comparison:**

```python
import numpy as np

r = np.array([0.010, 0.011, 0.0105, 0.0115, 0.011, 0.012, 0.0115, 0.0125])
# Incorrect: multiply mechanically by sqrt(250)
naive_ann_vol = float(np.std(r, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(250.0))

# Example assumption: returns follow an AR(1) process with known phi=0.3
# Long-run variance inflation includes every lag rho_k = phi**k
phi = 0.3
if not -1.0 < phi < 1.0:
    raise ValueError("AR(1) coefficient must lie strictly between -1 and 1")
inflation = (1.0 + phi) / (1.0 - phi)
adjusted_ann_vol = float(naive_ann_vol * np.sqrt(inflation))
assert inflation > 0.0 and adjusted_ann_vol > naive_ann_vol
```

The exact finite-$A$ variance of a sum is
$$\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{t=1}^A r_t\right)
=A\sigma^2\left[1+2\sum_{k=1}^{A-1}(1-k/A)\rho_k\right].$$
This counts the $A-k$ pairs at lag $k$. For an AR(1), $\rho_k=\phi^k$; only as a long-span approximation can the infinite sum $1+2\sum_{k\ge1}\phi^k=(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)$ replace it. The code above uses this long-run approximation, not the exact 250-period formula. For a general process, $\rho_1>0$ alone does not determine the sign of the full weighted sum.

- **Numerical difference:** With AR(1) parameter $\phi=0.3$, the long-run variance inflation factor is $(1+0.3)/(1-0.3)\approx1.857$, giving a standard-error inflation factor of 1.363. In empirical work, estimate $\phi$ on the training sample and report its uncertainty. For a general correlated series, use all relevant autocorrelation lags or a HAC variance estimate; a single sample $\rho_1$ is not an exact long-run variance correction.

### 12.2 Error 2: Substituting the Total Number of Strategy Columns $M$ for the Number of Independent Trials $N$ in DSR

- **Example of the error and why it occurs:** If 200 strategies are only small parameter variations, the number of columns may not equal the number of independent searches. Average Pearson correlation is also insufficient to determine the maximum-value distribution. It does not identify an exact “true independent trial count” or guarantee the direction of an adjustment. The following compares the numerical output of one explicitly stated heuristic.
- **Executable comparison:**

```python
import math
from quant_course.inference import deflated_sharpe_ratio, implied_independent_trials

# Observed daily Sharpe 0.15, variance 0.002, total trials M=200
m_trials = 200
# Incorrect: treat 200 columns as 200 independent trials
dsr_naive, sr0_naive = deflated_sharpe_ratio(0.15, 0.002, m_trials, 1250)

# Heuristic sensitivity: interpolate N_hat using average correlation rho=0.5
n_hat = implied_independent_trials(m_trials, avg_corr=0.5)
dsr_heuristic, sr0_heuristic = deflated_sharpe_ratio(0.15, 0.002, int(round(n_hat)), 1250)

assert sr0_naive > sr0_heuristic
assert dsr_heuristic > dsr_naive
```

- **Numerical difference:** With average correlation 0.5, the heuristic gives noninteger $\hat N=0.5+0.5\times200=100.5$. The current API accepts integer $N$, so the example uses Python’s `round(100.5) == 100` as an approximation. The API also clips the interpolation to $[1,M]$; a value above $M$ from negative correlation is returned as $M$. This is an implementation policy, not an extreme-value calibration theorem. The discretization does not change the fact that the formula is a heuristic or imply that its adjusted threshold establishes true alpha.

### 12.3 Error 3: Confusing the Arithmetic Mean with the Geometric Growth Rate

- **Example of the error and why it occurs:** Treating the simple arithmetic mean of period returns as compound performance ignores volatility drag.
- **Executable comparison:**

```python
import numpy as np

# Two-period symmetric returns: +50% and -50%
r_seq = np.array([0.50, -0.50])
arith_mean = float(r_seq.mean())
geom_mean = float(np.sqrt(np.prod(1.0 + r_seq)) - 1.0)

assert arith_mean == 0.0
assert round(geom_mean, 6) == -0.133975
```

- **Numerical difference:** The arithmetic mean is 0.00%, but ending wealth is only 75% of initial capital (a geometric average return of −13.3975%). High volatility erodes compound growth and can hide bankruptcy risk behind an arithmetic-average illusion.

## 13. Quantitative Research Application

### A Complete Performance-Inference Workflow: From Trial Ledger to Capital-Allocation Gate

An auditable quantitative-research workflow can be organized into four steps. Choose thresholds in advance for the decision at hand; PSR, DSR, and PBO do not have a universal launch threshold outside a model and risk preference.

```text
[Step 1: Register the trial ledger]
  Record each parameter change and factor addition/removal in the TrialLedger (Chapter 22)
  Record total trials M, sample length T, and evaluation frequency
        ↓
[Step 2: Audit descriptive measures]
  Calculate CAGR, MaxDD, Calmar, volatility, hit rate, and Profit Factor
  Connect to the Chapter 29 ledger to audit turnover and slippage costs
        ↓
[Step 3: Account for non-normality and single-test significance (PSR)]
  Estimate sample skewness gamma3 and kurtosis gamma4
  Calculate PSR(0) at the native frequency and interpret it against a prespecified one-sided significance level
        ↓
[Step 4: Adjust for selection and multiple trials (DSR + CSCV)]
  Record dependence structure; if the effective number of trials is unknown, disclose the unknown and a sensitivity range instead of forcing an exact N_hat
  Calculate the expected maximum random-noise threshold SR0 and report DSR with its assumptions, frequency, and trial count
  Run CSCV with S blocks; report PBO, loss fraction, and partition performance, using thresholds set in advance for the decision
```

The transition from Chapter 32 to Chapter 33 is explicit: PSR, DSR, and PBO determine how to interpret performance evidence; the next chapter uses actual positions to calculate losses, exit capacity, and limits. Strong evidence does not replace risk constraints, and a search diagnostic does not directly generate a sell order.

## 18. Solutions and Solution Hints

### 32-E01 — Suggested Answer

1. **Conditions for scaling:** For the sum of per-period excess returns, if the observations are i.i.d. with finite variance, then $E[\sum_{t=1}^A r_t]=A\mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\sum_{t=1}^A r_t)=A\sigma^2$. The Sharpe ratio of the sum is therefore $\sqrt A\,\mu/\sigma$. Normality is not required for this square-root scaling; it is an additional distributional assumption for classical normal-based confidence intervals or tests. With compounded simple returns, annual wealth is a product rather than the sum of periodic returns, so square-root annualization is usually a convention or approximation.
2. **Bias from positive autocorrelation:** For a stationary weakly dependent series, the variance of the sample mean scales with the long-run variance. Under an AR(1), the asymptotic factor is $1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\phi^k=(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)$. Positive first-order autocorrelation in an AR(1) makes this factor greater than 1. Using $\sqrt A$ anyway understates the standard deviation of an aggregated return and therefore overstates annualized Sharpe. For a general series, inference should use the full autocorrelation structure or a HAC standard error; $\rho_1$ alone does not determine the long-run variance.

### 32-E02 — Suggested Answer

The denominator variance term is
$$D=1-\hat{\gamma}_3\hat{\mathrm{SR}}+
\frac{\hat{\gamma}_4-1}{4}\hat{\mathrm{SR}}^2.$$
For $\hat{\mathrm{SR}}>0$:

- $\frac{\partial D}{\partial\hat{\gamma}_3}=-\hat{\mathrm{SR}}<0$. Thus, as skewness becomes more negative ( $\hat{\gamma}_3$ decreases), $D$ increases monotonically.
- $\frac{\partial D}{\partial\hat{\gamma}_4}=\hat{\mathrm{SR}}^2/4>0$. Thus, as kurtosis increases (heavier tails), $D$ increases monotonically.

The statistic is
$$Z=\frac{(\hat{\mathrm{SR}}-\mathrm{SR}^*)\sqrt{n-1}}{\sqrt D}.$$
If observed Sharpe is above the threshold, an increase in $D$ lowers $Z$ and lowers PSR under the standard normal CDF $\Phi$. If observed Sharpe is below the threshold, the negative $Z$ moves toward zero and PSR rises toward 0.5. If observed Sharpe equals the threshold, PSR is unchanged at 0.5. This calibrates estimation error in a threshold comparison.

### 32-E03 — Suggested Answer

By the asymptotic theory of standard normal extremes, as $N\to\infty$, the maximum of $N$ independent standard normal variables has the expansion
$$E[\max(Z_1,\dots,Z_N)]\sim
\sqrt{2\ln N}
-\frac{\ln\ln N+\ln(4\pi)}{2\sqrt{2\ln N}}
+\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{2\ln N}}.$$
Since $\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt{2\ln N}=+\infty$, the expected maximum noise value grows without bound even when the strategy pool consists entirely of zero-mean white noise. This is a statement about an expectation under the model; it does not guarantee that every finite search produces a specified high value. Nor does the Gaussian approximation make the sample Sharpe ratio’s exact distribution Gaussian.

### 32-E04 — Suggested Answer

For $N=3$ and $\bar r^*=2$:

1. **Definition 2.2** uses the condition $\bar r^*<N/2=1.5$. Since $2\ge1.5$, this partition is **not counted as overfit**.
2. **Algorithm 2.3** gives relative rank $\bar\omega=2/(3+1)=0.50$ and Logit $\lambda=\ln(0.5/0.5)=0$. Its integral convention includes partitions with $\lambda\le0$, so this partition **is counted as overfit**.

The two rules use different thresholds: strict $r<N/2$ and CSCV $r\le(N+1)/2$. At $N=4$, the first counts only rank 1 and the second counts ranks 1 and 2. At $N=10$, they count ranks 1–4 and ranks 1–5, respectively. The difference also occurs for even $N$; choose and disclose the convention before looking at results.

### 32-P01 — Reference Implementation and Checks

```python
import math
from quant_course.inference import sortino_ratio

def independent_sortino(returns, target=0.0, periods_per_year=1.0):
    x = [float(v) for v in returns]
    if not x or not all(math.isfinite(v) for v in x):
        raise ValueError("returns must be nonempty and finite")
    if not math.isfinite(target) or not math.isfinite(periods_per_year) or periods_per_year <= 0:
        raise ValueError("target and annualization must be finite; annualization positive")
    downside = math.sqrt(sum(min(v-target, 0.0)**2 for v in x) / len(x))
    if downside == 0:
        raise ValueError("downside deviation is zero")
    return (sum(x)/len(x)-target) / downside * math.sqrt(periods_per_year)

r = [0.04, 0.0, -0.02, 0.03, -0.01, 0.05, -0.03]
expected_dd = math.sqrt(0.0014 / 7)
expected = (0.06 / 7) / expected_dd * math.sqrt(12)
value = independent_sortino(r, periods_per_year=12)
assert math.isclose(value, expected, rel_tol=1e-12)
assert math.isclose(value, sortino_ratio(r, target_return=0.0, periods_per_year=12), rel_tol=1e-12)
assert not math.isclose(expected_dd, math.sqrt(0.0014 / 3))
for values, target in [([0.0, 0.02], 0.0), (r, math.nan), (r, math.inf), (r, -math.inf)]:
    try:
        independent_sortino(values, target)
    except ValueError:
        pass
    else:
        raise AssertionError("undefined/nonfinite case must fail")
print(value, expected_dd)
```

The zero-return observation remains in the full-sample denominator $n=7$; the denominator must not be changed to 3, the number of negative returns. For a nonzero target, subtract the same-frequency target from each observation first. Undefined cases should raise an error. The non-finite-target guard in the shared API is checked separately by a direct test of the candidate implementation.

### 32-C02 — Challenge Argument and Rubric

Let $X$ take values −3, −1, 1, and 3 with probability $1/4$ each, so its mean is 0 and variance is 5. Construct three joint distributions with the same complete marginals:

1. **Joint distribution with the larger maximum:** First choose a mapping independently: with probability $5/8$, use the adjacent swap $g=(-1,-3,3,1)$; with probability $3/8$, use the reversal $h=-X$. Then let $Y$ be the chosen mapping applied to $X$. Its joint probability matrix (rows for $X$ and columns for $Y$, each ordered as −3, −1, 1, 3) is
   $$P_H=\frac1{32}\begin{pmatrix}
   0&5&0&3\\
   5&0&3&0\\
   0&3&0&5\\
   3&0&5&0
   \end{pmatrix}.$$
   Every row and column sums to $8/32=1/4$. Under the adjacent swap,
   $E[Xg(X)]=(3+3+3+3)/4=3$; under reversal, $E[X(-X)]=-5$. The mixture covariance is therefore $(5/8)3+(3/8)(-5)=0$, so the Pearson correlation is zero. But
   $E[\max(X,g(X))]=(-1-1+3+3)/4=1$, while the maximum under reversal has expectation $E|X|=2$. Therefore $E[\max(X,Y)]=5/8+6/8=11/8$.
2. **Independent joint distribution:** Each of the 16 cells has probability $1/16$. The maximum has probabilities $(1,3,5,7)/16$ at the four support points, so its expectation is $(-3-3+5+21)/16=5/4$; covariance is zero.
3. **Joint distribution with the smaller maximum:** Choose $Y=X$ or $Y=-X$ with probability $1/2$ each. Each of the four cells associated with either mapping has probability $1/8$, and the marginals remain uniform. Covariance is $(5-5)/2=0$. The maximum has expectation $\tfrac12E[X]+\tfrac12E|X|=1$.

For $M=2$ and average correlation 0, the interpolation gives $\hat N=2$ in all three cases but cannot distinguish expected maxima of $11/8$, $5/4$, and 1. The independent model understates the maximum by $1/8$ in the first example and overstates it by $1/4$ in the third. Nonlinear dependence alone does not determine the direction. This is a teaching counterexample with a finite four-point marginal; it is not an exact Gaussian DSR calibration. Pearson correlation measures linear association for any variables with finite second moments, not only elliptically distributed variables, but does not characterize the full joint tail. When $M>T$, a demeaned sample matrix has rank at most $T-1$, so its correlation matrix can be singular. PCA or mutual-information dimensions can describe dependence, but are not exact trial counts without extreme-value calibration.

**Rubric:** 40 points for the complete marginals, joint probabilities, and moment calculations; 40 points for the three expected maxima and direction comparisons; 20 points for explaining the marginal model and DSR applicability boundary.

### 32-C04 — Challenge Argument and Rubric

- **Suggested argument and reproducible simulation:** A $t_4$ distribution has finite variance but an infinite population fourth moment. Therefore, the PSR asymptotic variance formula, which assumes a finite fourth moment, has no established calibration guarantee for $t_4$. The code below uses variance-standardized returns from each degrees-of-freedom distribution under a zero-mean null, estimates sample Sharpe, skewness, and Pearson raw kurtosis, and counts each simulation with $\mathrm{PSR}(0)>0.95$ as a rejection. This experiment compares the plug-in rule in finite samples; its rejection rates are not interpreted as a valid significance level under $t_4$.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(20261007)
reps = 20_000
observed_counts = []
for n in [24, 60, 120, 240]:
    for label, df in [("Normal", None), ("t5", 5), ("t4", 4)]:
        x = rng.normal(size=(reps, n)) if df is None else rng.standard_t(df, size=(reps, n)) * np.sqrt((df - 2) / df)
        sr = x.mean(axis=1) / x.std(axis=1, ddof=1)
        g3 = stats.skew(x, axis=1, bias=False)
        g4 = stats.kurtosis(x, axis=1, fisher=False, bias=False)
        d = 1 - g3 * sr + (g4 - 1) * sr**2 / 4
        assert np.all(d > 0)
        psr = stats.norm.cdf(sr * np.sqrt(n - 1) / np.sqrt(d))
        rejected = int(np.count_nonzero(psr > 0.95))
        observed_counts.append(rejected)
        print(n, label, rejected, rejected / reps)
np.testing.assert_array_equal(observed_counts, [935, 1267, 1280, 968, 1116, 1191, 1002, 1109, 1158, 1024, 1039, 1096])
```

|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 24 | 4.675% (935/20000) | 6.335% (1267/20000) | 6.400% (1280/20000) |
| 60 | 4.840% (968/20000) | 5.580% (1116/20000) | 5.955% (1191/20000) |

Sample kurtosis is always a finite number in these simulations, but that does not restore the population moment condition for $t_4$. Values near 5% and their changes with $n$ describe only this Monte Carlo configuration; they do not give a general “number of months required for calibration” threshold.
